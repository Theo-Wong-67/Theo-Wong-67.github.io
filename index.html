I am an MSc Biomedical Engineering student in the Neurotechnology stream at Imperial College London, with a First Class BEng in Biomedical Engineering from the University of Reading. My work so far has been in neural signal processing and computational modelling of neural systems. For my MSc project I modelled how grid cells lose their spatial firing pattern in Alzheimer's disease with a continuous attractor network, calibrated the analysis against published mouse recordings, and swept synaptic damage against velocity noise across roughly a hundred thousand simulations. Before that I built a causal decoder of hand position from motor cortex spike trains, and a filtering and classification pipeline for pathological ECG. What the three have in common is taking a neural or physiological signal, turning it into a model or an estimate, and then testing if the estimate can be used to distinguish or relevant pathology.

Research Interests

So far my work has been on decoding and modelling neural activity. What I want to do next is apply the same tools to signals from a person using a system, whether that is a BMI, a prosthesis or an XR interface, instead of from an experiment.

  • Computational neuroscience

    I want to describe what the brain does as mathematics, so that observations of a brain function become a model that can be run and compared with recordings. The grid cell project is my first attempt at this, and what I want to keep doing is finding measures that can be computed on both the model and the recordings, so that the comparison between them is fair before it is favourable.

  • Motor learning in interactive systems

    I have not built an interactive system yet and am learning Unity and C# at the moment. What I want to find out is whether the learning mechanisms I have learnt can be used inside a system that a person practises on, so that the task changes with how their control changes.

  • Biosignal processing and decoding

    Most of what I have built takes a signal recorded from the body, spike trains, ECG, and turns it into usable features for classifiers or regressors, drawing interpretable estimates out of noisy recordings. I want to keep working on this because the filtering and decoding steps decide how much of the signal is actually left, as I think there is potential information in the signals that are being filtered out or left unused.

Research Projects

My past projects and what I learnt from them.

Modelling grid cell network under Alzheimer's Disease

2026 · MSc individual project, supervised by Prof. Simon Schultz and Jess Yu, Imperial College London

A continuous attractor model of grid cells (Burak and Fiete, 2009) with two damage mechanisms, a patch of weakened synapses and noise on the velocity input, as a proxy for neurodegeneration in Alzheimer's disease. My Fourier symmetry pipeline reproduces the published mouse data (Ying et al., 2023) to within about 2% component-count deviation, and the model was then swept over damage and noise using HPC to map where grid symmetry breaks.

Brain-machine interface neural decoder

2026 · Brain Machine Interfaces module, Imperial College London

A causal MATLAB pipeline that decodes reaching direction and hand trajectory from 98 monkey motor neurons. Kernel-smoothed firing rates in 20 ms bins go into a regularised LDA classifier for direction (97.54% accuracy), then into one of eight direction-specific Kalman filters for position (13.2 cm RMSE over 50/50 cross-validation).

Filtering effects on pathological ECG classification

2024 · BEng dissertation, supervised by Prof. Sillas Hadjiloucas, University of Reading

Four filtering methodologies were compared on a synthetic noisy ECG, then arrhythmia was classified on MIT-BIH recordings with PCA, k-means and an RBF SVM (97.85% accuracy), and the classifier was re-run under different noise, filtering and sampling rates to see what it was sensitive to.
